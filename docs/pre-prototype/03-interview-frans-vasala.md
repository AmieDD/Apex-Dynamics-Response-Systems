## Interview details

* Participant: Frans Vasala
* Role: Incident command representative
* Interviewer: Kadijah Batlouni, Apex service designer
* Date: February 12, 2030
* Duration: 31 minutes

## Transcript excerpt

**Kadijah:** Walk me through the last time you could not trust the operational picture.

**Frans:** We had what looked like two inbound tracks. One implied landfall near a
dense waterfront district. The other bent north toward industrial land. If they were
the same animal, we were looking at sensor drift. If they were different animals, every
resource plan was too small.

**Kadijah:** What did you see first?

**Frans:** A severity notice in the regional queue, then a map from the marine desk,
then three chat messages asking whether the notice was real. The order mattered. By the
time the map appeared, people had already anchored on the severity label.

**Kadijah:** What did the severity label let you do?

**Frans:** It gave everyone a common shorthand. That matters. I can say "condition
four" on a crowded channel and people understand the posture. The danger is that the
number sounds settled when the evidence behind it is not.

**Kadijah:** What did you do next?

**Frans:** Asked intelligence to reconcile the tracks, asked dispatch to stage without
committing, and called the field branch about routes. Those happened in parallel. Our
systems showed them as separate activities, so I kept the combined state in my head.

**Kadijah:** What was hardest to keep in your head?

**Frans:** What had changed and who knew it. I could remember the facts. I could not
always remember whether dispatch had heard the correction or whether the north branch
was still acting on the earlier boundary.

**Kadijah:** When do you want detail, and when do you want a summary?

**Frans:** Summary first. I need the shape of the incident in seconds. Detail when the
summary changes a decision or when two sources disagree. If every caveat is equally
prominent, the screen becomes another report.

**Kadijah:** Who should decide what is prominent?

**Frans:** That is where I become cautious. Command owns the decision, but command
does not own every source. The marine specialist knows whether a bearing is plausible.
The field leader knows whether a road exists in practice. A system should not turn my
rank into data expertise.

**Kadijah:** What would failure look like?

**Frans:** The system gives us a clean answer, we move quickly, and nobody notices
that the answer excluded the one person with contradictory evidence.

**Kadijah:** What would success look like?

**Frans:** I spend less time asking everyone to repeat the current picture and more
time deciding what risk we are willing to take.

**Kadijah:** Would one shared display accomplish that?

**Frans:** It might. A shared display can also create shared confidence in the wrong
thing. I would want to test how disagreement survives the summary.

**Kadijah:** What should Apex prototype first?

**Frans:** Show me a messy incident in a form I can scan, then let me challenge it.
Do not automate dispatch. Do not make the visual neatness stronger than the evidence.

## Researcher note

Frans repeatedly distinguishes command authority from source expertise. His support
for a concise shared picture is conditional on visible disagreement and challenge.
