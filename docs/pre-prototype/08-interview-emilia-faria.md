## Interview details

* Participant: Emilia Faria
* Role: Accessibility representative
* Interviewer: Kadijah Batlouni, Apex service designer
* Date: February 19, 2030
* Duration: 30 minutes

## Transcript excerpt

**Kadijah:** We are considering a visual command-center prototype. What should we test
early?

**Emilia:** Start with the assumption hidden in "visual." Who must use it, from what
distance, under what lighting, with what input method, and while doing what else?

**Kadijah:** The sponsor described a wallboard that operators could also use at a desk.

**Emilia:** Those are different contexts. A wallboard supports shared orientation. A
desk tool supports action and inspection. Text that reads well three feet away may
fail across the room. A dense display that looks efficient on a large monitor may
become unusable when zoomed.

**Kadijah:** Are colors useful for threat conditions?

**Emilia:** Yes, as one cue. Do not make red, amber, and green carry the meaning alone.
Use labels, ordering, and language that remains understandable in forced colors and
grayscale.

**Kadijah:** What about incoming signals?

**Emilia:** A constantly updating feed can overwhelm anyone, not only screen-reader
users. Decide which changes deserve interruption. Preserve a way to review what
arrived without forcing attention to jump.

**Kadijah:** What do you need to know before recommending a design?

**Emilia:** Which tasks must be completed without a mouse, which information is
safety-critical, whether operators use magnification, whether audio cues compete with
radio traffic, and what happens during fatigue or high stress.

**Kadijah:** We may have only a few weeks for the first artifact.

**Emilia:** Then keep it honest. A prototype can include basic semantic structure,
keyboard operation, readable contrast, and reduced-motion behavior without claiming
accessibility. More important, do not let a polished visual demo lock the team into a
layout before representative users can challenge it.

**Kadijah:** What is the risk if accessibility comes later?

**Emilia:** The team may treat the visible arrangement as the product's logic. Then
changing reading order, grouping, focus, or scale feels like redesign instead of
learning.

**Kadijah:** What would success look like for this phase?

**Emilia:** The prototype makes its assumptions testable and does not exclude people
from the test by construction.

## Researcher note

Emilia distinguishes shared wallboard awareness from individual interaction. She warns
that visual polish can harden an untested information hierarchy.
